Scroll-driven · published gauge records

Every flood peak is a number somebody had to act on before it arrived.

This page reads the same set of published Australian flood observations six different ways. Scroll to move through them; the chart re-sorts and morphs rather than being replaced, so a river you are following stays findable between views.

Gauge heights, rainfall totals, dam storages and dwelling counts are as published by the Bureau of Meteorology, state agencies, councils and the reports cited at the foot of this page. Arrival-time interpolation between published observations is modelled. Hover any element for its exact value and coordinates.

01 — CHRONOLOGICAL

A century of record peaks, in the order they happened.

Seventeen gauge records across four states. The vertical axis is metres on the river gauge; the horizontal axis is the year of the peak. Nothing is sorted yet, so the pattern you see is only the pattern of when rivers happened to flood.

The tallest mark on the page is the Mary River at Gympie, 22.96 m in February 2022 W22, the highest Mary River level in over a century.

02 — RANKED BY MAGNITUDE

Re-sorted by height, the chronology dissolves.

The same seventeen marks, now ordered largest to smallest. Watch each one travel to its new position rather than reappearing there — the point of the morph is that you can follow a single river across the sort.

Ranking by raw gauge height flatters big rivers with deep channels. Gympie at 22.96 m outranks Lismore at 14.37 m ABC-L, but Lismore is the one where the levee was overtopped by 4.37 m and the CBD was cut off.

03 — RANKED BY EXCEEDANCE

Metres above the major flood level is the number that matters.

Each gauge is re-scaled against its own published major flood level, so the bars now read as how far past the town's own definition of a major flood the river went. The order changes completely.

Lismore exceeded its major level by 4.67 m; Gympie by 10.96 m; Ipswich by 5.02 m W22. This is the ranking an operations centre would work from, not the raw-height one.

04 — SHARE BY EVENT

Four events, and how the load was distributed inside each.

The marks collapse into stacked bars, one per event, each segment a gauge sized by its exceedance. The stack shows where an event's pressure concentrated: the 2022 south-east Queensland and Northern Rivers floods spread across seven gauges in two states; Townsville 2019 concentrated on one dam.

Ross River Dam reached 43.00 m, 247% of capacity TCC, having received 932,748 ML of catchment inflow TCC-D.

05 — AGAINST THE PREVIOUS RECORD

Which peaks broke the record they were measured against.

Each gauge is plotted against its own previous record: horizontal axis the old mark, vertical axis the new one. Anything above the diagonal is a new record. Anything below it was survivable by living memory.

Lismore's 14.37 m broke the 12.11 m standing since 1954 and 1974 SES-L. Brisbane's 3.85 m stayed below 2011's 4.46 m and well below 1974's 5.45 m BCC — and still inundated 23,400 homes across 177 suburbs W22.

06 — RAINFALL THAT ARRIVED FIRST

The rain totals that preceded each peak, scattered then gathered.

Before any gauge moved, rain fell. These are the published catchment and station totals, released from their positions and re-gathered by magnitude.

Townsville Aero recorded 1,259.8 mm over ten days to 8 February 2019 AIDR-T, beating the 925.5 mm standing since 1953. The Brisbane River catchment averaged 444.6 mm in four days BOM-B, breaking the 397.4 mm of 1974. At Rock Valley above Lismore, 503 mm fell in 24 hours AIDR-N.
